Overview & Rationale

Why proper specimen handling matters — error data, categories of loss, and the 6-step chain of custody every HCW must know.

🎯
Purpose: Proper collection, labeling, preservation, transportation, and chain of custody ensures continuity of quality care and provides a method to retrieve needed information. Every HCW in the chain of custody must understand the procedures and the consequences of errors.

Two Categories of Specimen Loss

Category 1 — Repeatable
Procedure Can Be Repeated
Still not optimal. Patient must repeat the risk of undergoing a procedure again, increasing morbidity, loss of time from job and family, and mental stress and anxiety. Healthcare facility faces possible litigation.
Category 2 — Irreplaceable ⚠️
Specimen Cannot Be Replaced
Greatest concern. Includes excised organs or tumors. Incomplete diagnosis, misdiagnosis, or delayed treatment can result in wrong treatment, unnecessary additional treatment, or treatment of the wrong body area. Litigation risk for the facility.

The Preanalytic Phase — 6-Step Chain of Custody

🔗
Preanalytic Phase: All specimen handling before it reaches the pathology department. Involves many HCWs and many steps — each with additional sub-steps that increase the risk of error.
1
Correctly Identify the Patient
Patient identification is the first and foundational step before any specimen activity.
2
Correctly Identify and Confirm the Specimen
The surgical team confirms the specimen by verbal communication — surgeon ↔ CST ↔ circulator.
3
Place in Appropriate Container and Preservative
Correct container type, size, and preservative must be selected based on the specimen type and surgeon's orders.
4
Correctly Label the Specimen
Label placed on the container side (not lid) in permanent black or blue ink by the circulator in the OR.
5
Complete the Pathology Requisition Slip
Pre-printed form accompanies every specimen. Contains patient identifiers, clinical diagnosis, study requested, surgeon name, and circulator name.
6
Transport to the Pathology Department
Specimen is transported in a secure, non-transparent, impervious container. Chain of custody is maintained and documented throughout.

Standards I – IV

Preoperative planning, proper containers, handling precautions, and labeling requirements.

Standard I
Proper handling begins in the preoperative phase — all communications should involve the surgical team.
▼

Preoperative Coordination Checklist

  • Type of specimen — e.g., frozen section, aerobic or anaerobic culture, specimen in preservative
  • Confirm diagnostic studies with surgeon — Gram stain, acid fast, mycological, etc.
  • Anticipated number of specimens
  • Container type and size — sterile or clean as appropriate
  • Preservative — will it be required? What type?
Cultural Preferences: The surgical team must be aware of any cultural or religious preferences regarding the specimen (e.g., certain religions require amputated body parts be returned to the patient). This must be documented in the patient's chart. If a patient requests to keep a specimen, it must still undergo pathological examination before being returned, per facility P&P and standard precautions.

Chain of Custody Communication

  • Notify the pathology department of procedures that will have surgical specimens
  • Communicate which procedures will require a frozen section
  • Alert HCWs in the chain of custody to any special circumstances — e.g., frozen section needing immediate delivery, or anaerobic culture requiring immediate transport to the laboratory
Standard II
Specimens should be placed in the proper container to preserve and protect them.
▼
Container Requirements
Physical Properties
  • Rigid — structurally sound
  • Impermeable — no leakage
  • Unbreakable — protects the specimen during transport
  • Non-reactive to fixative solutions — prevents HCW exposure to infectious substances
  • Secure, tight-fitting lid — prevents fixative escape and noxious fume release
Additional Requirements
Size, Inventory & Privacy
  • Correct size must be used to hold and protect the specimen
  • Healthcare facility must maintain an inventory of various sizes to accommodate all specimen types
  • Containers should be non-transparent — prevents visualization of contents during transport
  • Accompanying documentation must ensure patient confidentiality (HIPAA)
Standard III
HCWs should practice appropriate precautions when handling specimens and containers.
▼
  • Keep moist: Specimens must never be allowed to dry. Place in fixative ASAP, or keep in a sterile basin with sterile saline or saline-soaked sponges until placed in fixative
  • No radiopaque sponges: Specimens must NOT be passed off with or on a radiopaque counted sponge
  • Small specimens: Place on a sterile Telfa™ pad and pass off the sterile field using aseptic technique
  • Gentle handling by CST (first scrub role): When receiving from the surgeon and when passing off to the circulator
  • Avoid instruments if possible: Prevents crushing or damaging tissue. If a surgical instrument must be used, use an atraumatic clamp or tissue forceps
  • Do not crush manually: Avoid crushing the tissue with fingers when manually handling
  • Large specimens: May be placed in a sterile basin. Circulator places a towel over basin, puts in a clear impervious bag, and has specimen delivered to pathology immediately
  • CST must get surgeon permission before passing the specimen off the sterile field
  • Do not open after lidding: Once the lid is placed, do not open the container — risk of fixative spill and specimen contamination
Sterile Container Protocol: CST wipes outside of container with a clean sterile sponge before passing off. Circulator wears gloves when receiving. Circulator wipes outside with a clean sponge/towel and places in a clear, impervious bag.
Standard IV
Specimen containers must be properly labeled.
▼
🏷️
Who labels: The circulator writes on the label in the operating room. Use permanent black or blue ink only. Label placed on the side of the container — never the lid (lost when lid is removed in pathology).

Required Label Information (7 Items)

✓A. Type of specimen
✓B. Site of specimen including left or right side
✓C. Two unique identifiers e.g., patient name + hospital number
✓D. Date and time specimen received from CST
✓E. Type of preservative if used
✓F. Surgeon's name
✓G. Suture tag if present and placement, e.g., 12 o'clock or lower left quadrant
Biohazard Labeling: The container must be clearly labeled as biohazardous when containing a biohazardous substance to protect HCWs handling it and prevent contamination.

Standard V — Formalin & Preservatives

Handling, storage, spill response, and first aid for preservative solutions — with a focus on formalin (formaldehyde).

☣️
Formalin Hazards: Formaldehyde is dangerous if swallowed; harmful/irritant if inhaled; absorbed through skin causing irritation; causes eye irritation and possible corneal damage if splashed. There is sufficient evidence that formaldehyde can cause nasopharyngeal cancer in humans. Formalin is an EPA U-listed hazardous chemical.

General Preservative Rules

  • Healthcare facility must establish P&Ps per OSHA, EPA, and manufacturer recommendations
  • HCWs must be knowledgeable about facility policies and receive training on safe handling, PPE use, accident care, and spill cleanup
  • Use of preservative must be confirmed by the CST and circulator with the surgeon
  • Do not pour formalin directly over a specimen — use a pre-poured container to avoid splashing

Required PPE When Handling Formalin

🥽
Goggles (eye protection)
😷
Mask
🛡️
Face Shield (in addition to goggles — not a substitute)
🧤
Protective impervious gloves
🥼
Protective impervious clothing/cover apparel
💨
NIOSH-approved full facepiece respirator (if above OSHA PEL)
Hand Washing: Thoroughly wash hands and forearms after handling formalin, even if gloves were worn.

Formalin Storage Requirements

  • Designated area in the surgery department must be established for formalin storage
  • Storage room must be cool, dry, and well-ventilated with a negative pressure ventilation system and exhaust hood
  • Substances incompatible with formalin must not be stored in the same room
  • Containers must be clearly marked "formalin" with a chemical hazard label
  • MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheet) for formalin must be permanently maintained and easily accessible at all times for HCWs who may be exposed
  • OSHA and EPA regulations for storage must be followed

Spill Response Protocol

Large Spill
Immediately Leave and Report
  • HCW must immediately leave the area
  • Report to proper healthcare facility personnel
  • Designated HCWs isolate the area
  • No personnel without proper PPE allowed in the area
  • Immediately remove all ignition sources from area of spill
Small Spill
Don PPE — Neutralize — Clean
  • Don all PPE before approaching spill
  • Remove ignition sources from area immediately
  • Neutralize with sodium hydroxide
  • Use absorbent material to clean spill
  • Place absorbent material in a hazardous-labeled container for disposal per EPA regulations

Formalin First Aid Procedures

Exposure TypeImmediate Action
👁️ Splashed in Eye(s) Immediately flush the eye(s) with water, occasionally lifting upper and lower eyelids. Seek medical treatment.
🖐️ Skin Contact Immediately remove contaminated clothing (including shoes if necessary). Flush skin with water. Seek medical treatment.
💨 Excessive Inhalation Immediately remove HCW from exposure area to fresh air. Rescuer may need PPE if area is heavily contaminated. Administer oxygen via mask ASAP. If unconscious and not breathing, administer artificial respiration after removal from the exposure area.
EPA Classification: Formalin is a U-listed hazardous chemical regulated by the US Environmental Protection Agency. Disposal must comply with local, state, and federal laws.

Special Specimen Types

Standards VI–IX — Frozen sections, aerobic/anaerobic cultures (swab and syringe), and radioactive specimens.

Standard VI
Frozen Section Specimens — Handling, Transport & Communication
▼
❄️
Critical Rule: Frozen section specimens must be immediately passed off the sterile field and delivered to the pathology department. These specimens must NOT be placed in preservative solution.
  • Confirm preoperatively and intraoperatively with surgeon that the specimen is for frozen section (both CST and circulator)
  • Notify pathology department preoperatively of which procedures will have frozen section specimens
  • CST passes off the sterile field to circulator using aseptic technique; circulator wears gloves when receiving
  • Do NOT use a counted radiopaque sponge — use a Telfa™ pad, sterile towel, sterile container, or medicine cup
  • Do NOT place in preservative solution
  • Follow facility P&P for labeling and transport to pathology
Pathologist Communication: The pathologist must communicate directly with the surgeon regarding diagnosis. A verbal direct report must be recorded in the patient's OR record. The pathologist must be informed of the patient's level of consciousness before giving an oral report. If verbal communication is not possible, a written communication must include date, time, pathologist's signature, and be placed in the OR record.
Standard VII
Aerobic & Anaerobic Culture Specimens (Swab/Tube)
▼
  • Culture must be obtained using aseptic technique to prevent contamination of the inside of the culture tube and swab
  • If circulator dispensed the culture tube onto the sterile field, circulator must wear gloves when the CST passes it off
  • Circulator must decontaminate the exterior of the culture tube prior to transport to the laboratory
  • Follow facility P&P for labeling and transport
  • Immediately deliver to the laboratory department — no delays
Standard VIII
Anaerobic Culture Specimens — Syringe Aspiration
▼
When Needle Used
CST Removes Needle First
  • CST removes needle and places in sharps container on the sterile backtable
  • CST recaps the syringe
  • Passes off the sterile field using aseptic technique
When No Needle Used
Recap Before Passing
  • CST recaps the syringe before passing off the sterile field to prevent fluid from escaping
Do NOT decontaminate the exterior of the syringe — this may cause the pushing or pulling of the plunger, resulting in leakage or ejection of the fluid. Circulator wears gloves and places syringe in an impervious bag or container. Lab personnel must be notified that the inside of the bag/container is contaminated since the exterior of the syringe has not been decontaminated.
  • Circulator wears gloves when handling the syringe
  • Place syringe in an impervious bag or container for transport
  • Immediately deliver to the laboratory — no delays
  • Follow facility P&P for proper labeling
Standard IX
Radioactive Specimens — e.g., Sentinel Lymph Node with Technetium
▼
☢️
Primary Goal: Keep radiation exposure to HCWs as low as possible. Only HCWs documented by the Radiation Safety Officer (RSO) as trained are authorized to handle radioactive specimens.
  • Healthcare facility must develop written procedures for handling, labeling, transporting, storing, and disposing of radioactive specimens
  • The RSO (Radiation Safety Officer) is responsible for training surgical personnel and other HCWs; only RSO-documented HCWs may handle radioactive specimens
  • The RSO is responsible for developing all safety procedures
  • Container labeling must follow US Nuclear Regulatory Commission Regulations
  • Federal law: Labels indicating radioactive material must be removed prior to disposal of the container
  • Pathology requisition slip must include the name of the radioactive material — e.g., "sentinel lymph node – technetium injection"
  • Radioactive specimens must be immediately transported from OR to pathology — never left unattended in an unsecured area
  • Personnel transporting to pathology must wear gloves when handling the container

Standards X–XII — Evidence & Device Specimens

Defective medical devices, medical-legal evidence (bullets, knives), and specimen submission policies.

Standard X
Defective Medical Device Specimens — Reporting & Handling
▼
  • Defective medical device must be reported to the manufacturer per FDA policies and procedures; healthcare facility report completed and provided to manufacturer with reason for removal
  • Upon removal, device placed in an impervious bag or appropriate container; all parts must be kept together
  • Must NOT be decontaminated or sterilized prior to transport from the surgery department — doing so destroys evidence needed for investigation
  • Device transported to the pathology department to document confirmation of identity
Standard XI
Foreign Objects / Medical-Legal Evidence — Bullets, Knives & Chain of Custody
▼
⚖️
Legal Requirement: Foreign objects such as bullets are medical-legal evidence. Chain of custody must be properly documented from removal by surgeon to transfer to law enforcement.

Patient Clothing & Belongings

  • Handle with non-powdered gloves; handle clothing as little as possible to avoid particle loss
  • Cut clothing along the seams; avoid cutting through holes or punctures — they may indicate bullet or knife entry points
  • Clothing placed in paper bags (NOT plastic — plastic confines moisture, decomposing evidence and promoting mold/mildew)
  • Wet/bloody clothing placed in moisture-proof bags to prevent leaking and cross-contamination
  • Footwear in a separate paper bag
  • Stretcher sheets in a separate paper bag (evidence may have fallen from clothing)
  • Each bag labeled with patient name, contents, and numbered; documented in OR record
  • Document detailed descriptions of wounds, body markings, and any skin puncture sites by OR personnel (e.g., IV insertion site)
  • Document any statements made by the patient before or during induction, in detail

Bullet Handling Protocol

Do NOT handle bullets with metal instruments unless tip covers are placed by the CST first. Metal-on-metal can scratch the bullet surface and destroy evidence markings.
  • CST uses sterile water to rinse off blood and tissue — prevents microscopic evidence markings from being destroyed
  • CST places bullet in a nonmetal specimen container and passes off to circulator using aseptic technique
  • Circulator wears gloves, decontaminates outside of container, places in an evidence envelope, seals, and completes facility evidence document
  • Label must include: patient name and hospital number, date and time of evidence collection, surgeon's name

Knife Handling

  • To avoid smearing fingerprints, handle on areas not normally handled
  • Immediately placed in evidence envelope or bag by the surgeon upon removal

Transfer to Law Enforcement

  • Sealed evidence transferred directly to the law enforcement officer
  • OR record documentation must include: body area where removed, description of foreign body, officer name and badge number, time and method of transfer
  • If officer not immediately available, evidence secured in a locked location with only one key holder to guarantee chain of custody
Standard XII
Policies for Determining Pathology Submission Requirements
▼
  • Policies must be jointly formed by the pathologist and facility's clinical staff, per The Joint Commission Standards and College of American Pathologists recommendations
  • Policy must include that specimens not exempted from examination must be submitted to pathology
  • The surgeon and pathologist jointly have final decision-making authority regarding the degree of pathological examination
  • Policies and procedures for the disposition of tissue specimens must be based on local, state, and federal regulations
Category A
Not Required to Submit
Per facility policy — jointly established by pathologist and clinical staff per TJC and CAP recommendations.
Category B
Gross Examination Only
Visual/physical inspection only — no microscopic analysis required per policy.
Category C
Routine Microscopic Examination
Full pathological examination required. Specimens not exempted must always be submitted.

Standard XIII — Systems Approach & Documentation

A systems-based framework for identification, handling, communication, documentation, transportation, and disposition of specimens.

🔄
Systems Approach Philosophy: Rather than focusing blame on an individual, a systems approach views the handling process as a whole and establishes safeguards to reduce error risk. If an error occurs, policies and procedures can be reviewed and revised to prevent future system errors.

Key Systems Principles

A. Communication
Consistent Verbal & Written Methods
Establish consistent verbal and written communication methods and chain of custody. Develop a flowchart that can be periodically reviewed. Verbal confirmation between surgeon ↔ CST ↔ circulator at each hand-off.
B. Immediate Containment
Place & Label Immediately
Placing the specimen in its container and labeling it immediately reduces the risk of loss during OR cleanup. Verbal confirmation of patient and specimen information at each transfer point aids accuracy.
C. Pre-Printed Forms & Checklists
Reduce Reliance on Memory
Use checklists, laboratory requisition slips, and daily specimen logs. The circulator completes a checklist before the specimen leaves the OR ("Has the specimen been verified?" — each surgical team member verifies).
E. Chain of Custody Tracking
Minimize Hand-offs
Each hand-off is a potential error source — minimize the number. A surgery department specimen log book records: specimen name, two patient identifiers, date, time of log-in, and name of each HCW involved.
F. Transport Schedule
Established Pick-up Times
The pathology, laboratory, and surgery departments must agree on a set schedule for pick-up/transport of permanent specimens.
G. Designated Oversight
One Department Owns the System
A designated department (e.g., pathology) should oversee the specimen system, including development and revision of policies, to create ownership of the process.

CST / CSFA Competency Statements

Formal competency requirements and measurable criteria for Certified Surgical Technologists and Certified Surgical First Assistants.

🏅
Applies to: CSTs (Certified Surgical Technologists) and CSFAs (Certified Surgical First Assistants). Educational standards are established by the Core Curriculum for Surgical Technology and the Core Curriculum for Surgical Assisting.
Competency StatementMeasurable Criteria
1.CSTs and CSFAs have the knowledge and skills for the proper handling of all types of surgical specimens in the perioperative environment — including documentation, labeling, containment, storing, and transporting.
  • Educational standards per the Core Curriculum for Surgical Technology and Core Curriculum for Surgical Assisting
  • Care and handling of specimens included in CAAHEP- and ABHES-accredited surgical technology didactic studies, including documentation and legal/ethical aspects
2.CSTs and CSFAs understand and implement the policies and procedures for chain of custody of specimens.
  • Students demonstrate knowledge of specimen handling in lab/mock OR and during clinical rotation, including PPE use
3.CSTs and CSFAs have the knowledge and skills for implementing aseptic technique in the handling of specimens on and off the sterile field.
  • As practitioners, CSTs and CSFAs handle specimens per facility P&P. Where protocols allow, CSTs and CSFAs complete required documentation.
4.CSTs and CSFAs understand and implement EPA, CDC, and OSHA regulations for handling toxic preservative solutions.
  • CSTs and CSFAs complete continuing education to remain current on Standard Precautions and PPE
  • Annual review of facility policies on specimen handling
5.CSTs and CSFAs are knowledgeable of Standard Precautions and PPE use when handling specimens.
  • CSTs and CSFAs complete continuing education to remain current in their knowledge of Standard Precautions and PPE, as well as annual review of facility policies
6.CSTs and CSFAs understand the physical and psychological effects on the patient regarding loss, mishandling, or damage to a specimen, as well as the legal ramifications.
